\begin{abstract}
We apply hierarchical agglomerative clustering (HAC) on travel-time
distances -- under single linkage, mathematically equivalent to a
percolation sweep over the network's own edges -- to the Roman road
network, with the aim of recovering spatial units of interest directly
from network connectivity rather than from imposed administrative
boundaries. Before turning to our own network, we calibrate the method
on the exact network and travel-time model used in a recent large-scale
reconstruction of the Roman road system, and show that it recovers that
network's Roman provinces with an Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) of 0.70
(bootstrap 95\% CI [0.68, 0.71]) at a threshold of $\sim$77 hours
($\sim$3.2 days) of travel -- confirming that the method can recover a
known administrative partition when one exists in the data. We then
apply the same percolation-equivalent clustering to our own,
independently constructed network of the Roman road system, where the
goal is not to reproduce this calibration result but to characterize
the emergent spatial units the network itself suggests. A first,
coarse benchmark against the Roman provinces on this network gives a
markedly weaker alignment (ARI $\approx 0.43$) with a less sharply
defined optimal threshold, which we read not as a replication failure
but as a signal that provincial administration is only one of several
candidate organizing principles the clustering may be picking up --
motivating a closer characterization of these units, and a comparison
against spatial/temporal null models, as the next step of this work.
\end{abstract}

This paper is organized around two questions, both posed on the network
built from the Itiner-e Roman road data \citep{desoto2025itinere}:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Can hierarchical agglomerative clustering (HAC) on
  travel-time distances identify spatial units that emerge from the road
  network itself?} We use percolation-equivalent HAC (Section~\ref{sec:hac-method})
  to cluster the network at every travel-time threshold, calibrate the
  approach against a known partition (Section~\ref{sec:validation}), and
  then apply it to our own network to see what clusters it produces on
  its own terms (Section~\ref{sec:primary}).
\item \textbf{Are the spatial units that emerge significant, or are they
  artefacts of the network's own construction?} A cluster can look like a
  coherent region without reflecting anything beyond the geometry and
  density of the digitized road network it was built from. Answering this
  requires comparing the clusters obtained on the real network against
  the same procedure run on spatial and temporal null models of that
  network -- i.e.\ networks that share its nodes and broad connectivity
  but not whatever structure actually produces the clustering. This
  comparison, against the null models in \texttt{results/Null-models/},
  is the next stage of this work and is not yet part of this manuscript
  (see Section~\ref{sec:primary} and the Discussion).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Data}

The road network is built from the digitized linework released as the
Itiner-e dataset \citep{desoto2025itinere}, cleaned following the same
general procedure as \citet{pazout2026network}: near-miss junctions and
duplicate endpoints are snapped together, the linework is noded at every
intersection, and the resulting degree-two chains are simplified away,
giving an undirected planar graph whose nodes are road intersections and
dead ends and whose edges are the road segments between them. We use two
variants of this network. Our own, independently cleaned version has
8{,}460 nodes and 12{,}849 edges across 12 connected components, with
each edge weighted by travel time at a constant walking speed of
2~km/h. For calibration, we separately use the exact endpoint-noded
network and travel-time model of \citet{pazout2026network} themselves --
10{,}506 nodes and 14{,}770 edges across 13 connected components,
including two artificial crossings at the Bosporus and the Dardanelles
connecting the European and Asian landmasses, with travel time estimated
per edge from Tobler's hiking function \citep{tobler1993hiking} applied
to slope and geodesic length rather than a flat walking speed. Both
networks are overlaid with the same province boundaries: the Ancient
World Mapping Center's ``Roman Empire AD 200'' polygons, corrected by
A.\ Pa\v{z}out \citep{awmc2023provinces}, approximate and clipped to
modern coastlines, comprising 57 provinces with Italy further subdivided
into its constituent regiones for finer resolution on the Italian
peninsula -- a division not available for other provinces -- alongside a
coarser, 47-unit partition that merges the regiones back into a single
Italia for comparison. Each road edge is assigned to the province
containing its midpoint, falling back to the province with the longest
geometric overlap where the midpoint falls outside every polygon; nodes
inherit the province of their incident within-province edges, and edges
that cross a provincial boundary or cannot be assigned to any province
are excluded from the alignment scoring in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Hierarchical agglomerative clustering and percolation}
\label{sec:hac-method}

Hierarchical clustering under single linkage has a direct reading on a
weighted network: merging two clusters at the weight of the lightest
edge joining them means that, at any linkage height $t$, the clusters
are exactly the network's connected components once every edge heavier
than $t$ has been removed. Raising $t$ and cutting the dendrogram at
each height is therefore the same thing as percolating the network
itself, component by component, rather than clustering points by a
chosen distance metric. This is the basis of \citet{arcaute2016cities},
who percolate street intersections in Britain by Euclidean distance to
recover its regional and urban hierarchy, and of
\citet{molinero2017angular}, who percolate the roads themselves using
the relative angle between consecutive segments as the occupation cost.
We apply the same principle to travel time: within each connected
component we compute pairwise travel-time distances between nodes and
use single-linkage HAC, so that cutting the dendrogram at threshold $t$
recovers exactly the network's own travel-time percolation clusters at
that threshold. Our calibration network instead uses average linkage,
inherited from work that predates this percolation framing and kept
here as a deliberate point of comparison; it does not share the
guarantee above, so its cluster counts and province-alignment scores in
Section~\ref{sec:results} are not directly comparable to those of our
own network.

Sweeping $t$ across every merge height gives, at each threshold, the
number of clusters and the size of the largest one relative to the
network (the percolation curve), to which we fit a power law over its
emergent region. At each threshold $t$, we score the resulting clusters against the
province labels of nodes with a known province assignment using the
Adjusted Rand Index \citep[ARI;][]{hubert1985comparing}, and identify
the travel-time threshold at which this alignment is strongest.

\subsection{Entropy of cluster sizes}
\label{sec:entropy}

As clusters merge during the percolation sweep, the distribution of
cluster sizes changes shape. Following \citet{molinero2017angular}, we
plan to track the Shannon entropy of this distribution as a function of
threshold: local peaks in that entropy curve mark the points where the
system passes between qualitatively different stages of integration,
giving a more principled way to locate transitions in the percolation
process than reading them off the cluster-count curve by eye. This
analysis has not yet been carried out on either network; it is
introduced here as the next planned step.

\subsection{Comparison with spatial null models}
\label{sec:nullmodels}

To test whether the clusters found above reflect more than the
geometry of the network's node positions, we compare them against
spatial null models built on the same nodes but not the same roads: a
nearest-neighbour network and a Delaunay-triangulation network, each
reinforced with a minimum-spanning-tree backbone so that every node
remains reachable. Figure~\ref{fig:null_knn} and
Figure~\ref{fig:null_delaunay} show the same percolation procedure
applied to these two null networks. In both, the number of clusters
falls and the largest cluster grows smoothly and gradually with
threshold, without the plateaus visible in the real network's
percolation curve (Figure~\ref{fig:network0}). A full comparison,
scoring the real network's clusters against these null models threshold
by threshold, is still in progress, and will determine whether the
stages found in the real network are a genuine signal or an artefact of
its geometry alone.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Identifying spatial units using HAC}
\label{sec:validation}

Before asking what spatial units our own network suggests, we first
check that the method is capable of recovering a known partition at
all, on a network and travel-time model we did not build ourselves.
Figure~\ref{fig:network0} shows the percolation curve and
snapshot cluster maps for the calibration network of
\citet{pazout2026network}, with Tobler-function travel times and AWMC
provinces (10{,}430 of 10{,}506 nodes have a known province).

Table~\ref{tab:validation} reports ARI and NMI at six representative
travel-time snapshots. Alignment with the provinces rises from
essentially zero at 6--12 hours, peaks around 1--3 days, and falls off
again as clusters grow beyond provincial scale.

\centering
\caption{Province alignment at fixed travel-time snapshots, calibration
  network (\citet{pazout2026network}, Tobler-function travel time;
  $n=10{,}430$ nodes with known province).}
\label{tab:validation}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Snapshot & Clusters & ARI & NMI \\
\midrule
6 h   & 2{,}809 & 0.039 & 0.640 \\
12 h  & 1{,}376 & 0.092 & 0.683 \\
24 h  & 514     & 0.201 & 0.734 \\
2 d   & 154     & 0.516 & 0.803 \\
7 d   & 22      & 0.493 & 0.778 \\
12 d  & 15      & 0.308 & 0.689 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

An exhaustive search over every merge height finds a single, sharp peak
at $t = 77.1$ hours ($\sim$3.2 days), with ARI $= 0.695$ (95\% bootstrap
CI $[0.681, 0.710]$). The peak is stable: every bootstrap resample of
the node set selects the same 77-hour merge height as optimal. At this
threshold the network's emergent regions correspond closely to the
Roman provincial map.

\caption{Percolation curve and snapshot cluster maps for the calibration

Figure~\ref{fig:alignment_curve} shows ARI as a continuous function of
threshold rather than at the six fixed snapshots of
Table~\ref{tab:validation}, making the sharpness of the 77-hour peak
visible directly, and Figure~\ref{fig:province_match} breaks that peak
down province by province: for each province, how well its nodes stay
together in a single dominant cluster (recall) and how much of that
cluster is specific to it (precision), combined into an F1 score. Small,
geographically isolated provinces (Britannia, Cyprus, Creta, Sicilia,
Sardinia, Corsica) are recovered almost perfectly (F1 $\approx 1$), as
expected since islands and peripheral regions have little opportunity to
merge with neighbours at this threshold; the worst-recovered provinces
are the small Alpine provinces (Alpes Graiae, Cottiae, Poeninae,
Maritimae) and Germania Superior, which sit on travel corridors between
larger, better-connected neighbours and are absorbed into them rather
than forming distinct clusters of their own. This province-by-province
view is what we will be looking for an equivalent of in
Section~\ref{sec:primary}, once our own network's clusters have had the
same closer look.

\subsection{Applying HAC to the itinereX network}
\label{sec:primary}

Having confirmed in Section~\ref{sec:validation} that percolation-equivalent
HAC can recover a known administrative partition when one is present, we
turn to the first question posed in Section~\ref{sec:intro}: what spatial
units does the itinereX network itself suggest, under its own
independently built topology and a simple constant-speed (2~km/h)
travel-time model? This is not a replication of Section~\ref{sec:validation}
-- we are not trying to reproduce its ARI on a different network, and
treating the comparison that way would misread what each run is for.
Section~\ref{sec:validation} establishes that the method works; this
section applies it, for the first time, to a network nobody has clustered
before, with the goal of characterizing the clusters themselves as
candidate spatial units, of which provincial alignment is only one
possible interpretation among several worth testing. The second question
-- whether those units are significant, or artefacts of this particular
network's construction -- is addressed only partially here, and is taken
up properly in the paragraph after next.

As a first, coarse orientation, we benchmarked the resulting clusters
against the Roman provinces using the same ARI/NMI procedure as
Section~\ref{sec:validation}: an exhaustive search over every merge height
gives a best alignment of ARI $= 0.433$ (NMI $= 0.763$, 291 clusters) at
$t \approx 31.8$ hours, with a second, comparably good local optimum
around 25 hours rather than the single sharp peak seen in
Section~\ref{sec:validation} (95\% bootstrap CI on ARI $[0.426, 0.448]$;
on the threshold, $[24.8, 31.8]$ hours). We do not read this as a weaker
version of Section~\ref{sec:validation}'s result -- on a network built
under a different travel-time model, with its own topology, there is no
reason to expect the same number -- but as a preliminary signal that
provincial administration is detectable in, without fully explaining, the
clusters this network produces. We have deliberately not built a figure
or table around this number here: a single province-alignment statistic
does not do justice to what the clusters at each threshold actually look
like, and a closer, better-presented look at them is needed before drawing
conclusions from it.

The more interesting question is not why the ARI is lower here, but what
these clusters represent. That is exactly the second question raised in
Section~\ref{sec:intro}, and is taken up in
Sections~\ref{sec:entropy} and~\ref{sec:nullmodels}.
